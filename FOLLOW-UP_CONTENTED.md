# Stage 2 Live Interview Guide: The Contented Anomaly

**Cohort definition.** All four must hold:

- Currently active: B5 above $0, B8 = `Right now` or `Within the last month`
- Real scale: B7 = `6 to 20` or `More than 20`, **or** B5 at `$10K to $50K` or above
- No expressed pain in C2 or C6
- Not using an automation platform, or using one and content with it (D3 = `None` or D4 = `Yes, actively`)

**Expected volume: two or three people.** Possibly zero. If nobody in the sample fits all four, that is itself a finding worth noting, because it means every experienced manager who responded reported friction.

**Target 20 to 25 minutes.** Narrow probe, not a full discovery interview.

---

## What this interview is for

Not disconfirmation for its own sake. The specific job is to find out **why the burden everyone else reports is invisible to this person**, because there are only three possible explanations and all three matter:

1. **They have a workaround you cannot see.** A tool, a script, a service, a habit, a person they pay. This is competitive intelligence about a substitute that appears nowhere in the category data. Your real competitor is more likely a spreadsheet, a price alert, and a routine than it is Krystal.

2. **Their strategy structurally avoids the burden.** Wide ranges, stable pairs, full-range CPMM positions, set-and-forget deployments. If so, they are not in your market and the finding is about segmentation: the burden is a property of certain strategies, not of LPing.

3. **They have normalized a cost they stopped noticing.** They spend the hours and no longer register them as effort. This is the most common answer and the hardest to detect, because they will sincerely report no pain while describing a routine that consumes six hours a week.

Establishing which of the three is true is the entire interview. Everything below serves that.

---

## Ground rules

**Do not try to create the pain.** The failure mode here is leading them into agreeing that something is hard. If you succeed at that, you have destroyed the only genuinely independent data point in the study. Their contentment is the asset. Protect it.

**Never use the words automation, rebalance, burden, tedious, or frustrating.** Not once, at any point, until after the interview is over. If they use them, note the moment.

**Ask for the routine in operational detail, not for feelings.** "Walk me through Tuesday" gets you the truth. "Is it a lot of work" gets you a shrug.

**Count what they describe, do not ask them to count it.** If they narrate four checks a day, that is your number. Asking "how many hours does this take" invites them to produce a flattering estimate.

**Do not pitch at the end.** Unlike every other cohort. If word gets back to a community that you are building this, you lose the ability to recruit more unprimed contented managers. Thank them, ask for referrals, leave.

## Section 1: Establish that they are what the form said

- What are you running right now: positions, chains, pairs, sizes
- How long has that been in place
- Is this typical for you, or an unusually quiet period
- What is the busiest it has been in the last year

The quiet-period check matters. A manager between deployments will report no pain because there is currently nothing to manage. If that is the case, redirect the interview to the busiest period they described and treat them as an active manager instead.

## Section 2: The routine, in operational detail

This is the core section. Take your time and stay concrete.

- Walk me through the last time you touched a position. What prompted it, what did you do
- Walk me through yesterday, or the last day you did anything with these positions
- What is the first thing you check when you sit down
- What do you look at, on what screens
- How often does that happen in a day
- Does anything notify you, or do you go look
- What is the longest you have gone without checking
- Has anything ever needed attention while you were asleep, traveling, or at work? What happened
- Who else could do this if you could not

Then, only after they have narrated the routine:

- Adding that up, roughly how much of your week does this take

**Ask that last question only after they have described the routine, never before.** The gap between the routine you just counted and the number they give you is diagnostic. Someone who narrates four daily checks and then says "maybe an hour a week" is in explanation 3 and does not know it.

## Section 3: Hunting the workaround

- Is there anything you use to keep track of all this
- Did you set up any alerts, notifications, or automations of any kind
- Any spreadsheets, notes, dashboards, bots, scripts
- Did you build any of it, or find it
- Does anyone help you with this
- Do you pay for anything at all related to this, even indirectly
- Is there anything you use that was not built for this but that you use for this anyway
- What would break your process if it disappeared tomorrow

That last question is the sharpest one in the section. It surfaces load-bearing tools people forget to mention because they have stopped thinking of them as tools. A price alert on a phone, a Telegram bot, a browser tab that never closes.

## Section 4: Testing the structural explanation

- How wide are your ranges, typically
- Are your pairs correlated, stable, volatile
- How often does a position actually go out of range
- When it does, how quickly do you act, and does it matter if you are slow
- How long do you intend to hold a position when you open it
- Do you reopen positions or let them run
- Have you ever run something that needed more attention than what you run now? What was different

If ranges are wide, pairs are stable, and out-of-range events are rare, explanation 2 holds and the finding is clean: the burden is strategy-dependent, and your addressable market is narrower and more specific than "LPs."

The last question is the escape hatch. Someone who once ran something demanding and deliberately stopped has made a considered choice about effort, and the reasoning behind that choice is worth the whole interview.

## Section 5: The edges

- What is the most annoying part of this, even if it is minor
- Has this ever cost you money? What happened
- Is there anything you would do differently if it were free and instant to do
- Are you running fewer positions than you would like to
- Have you ever passed on an opportunity because of what it would take to manage
- Do you enjoy this part of it, or is it just what the returns require

Note that none of these ask whether it is hard. They ask what they have chosen not to do, which is where suppressed demand hides even in people who report satisfaction.

**The enjoyment question is the tiebreaker.** Someone who genuinely enjoys managing positions is not a customer at any level of product quality, and you should record that plainly rather than reading around it.

## Section 6: Close

- Do you know anyone who does this at a bigger scale than you? How do they handle it
- Has anyone ever asked you how you do it
- Anything I should have asked

No pitch. Thank them and end.

The first question is doing real work. Contented managers at moderate scale often know somebody operating one tier above them, and that person is frequently exactly who you want: same competence, more positions, and a burden that finally broke.

## Classifying the interview

Assign one of three, with the evidence:

| Classification           | Evidence                                                                                                             | Implication                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hidden substitute**    | Section 3 surfaced a tool, script, service, or person carrying the load                                              | Competitive intelligence. Add to your substitute list and treat it as a real competitor. Find out how many others use the same thing                                                                                                   |
| **Structural avoidance** | Section 4 shows wide ranges, stable pairs, rare out-of-range events, long holds                                      | Segmentation finding. The burden is a property of certain strategies. Narrow your ICP accordingly and stop counting this profile in your market size                                                                                   |
| **Normalized cost**      | Section 2 routine is heavy but self-reported hours are low, and Section 5 surfaces things they have chosen not to do | Real demand that is not consciously felt. Product has to make the cost visible before it can sell the cure. Same conclusion the cost-visibility question reaches in the frustrated active cohort, arriving from the opposite direction |

If two or three of these interviews all land on **structural avoidance**, that is the strongest and cleanest result this small cohort can produce, and it should tighten the ICP in the deck.

If they land on **hidden substitute** and the same substitute appears twice, stop and investigate it directly. Two independent mentions of an unlisted tool in a sample this small is a significant signal.

---

## Reading against the other cohorts

This cohort exists to be compared, not read alone.

- **Routine intensity.** Compare the operational routine described here against the frustrated actives. If they are similar in substance and differ only in how they are narrated, the variable is tolerance, not workload, and your product is selling to a temperament rather than a situation. That has real implications for market size.
- **Scale threshold.** Compare position counts and capital between this cohort and the frustrated actives. If contentment concentrates below a certain scale, you have found the threshold where the pain becomes real, and that number belongs in the deck.
- **Substitute inventory.** Anything surfaced in Section 3 goes on the competitive list alongside vfat and Krystal, and it probably belongs there ahead of them.

---

## Per-interview capture

- Cohort tag: `contented_anomaly`
- Positions, chains, pairs, capital range, strategy types
- Typical period or quiet period, and busiest period described
- Routine as narrated: checks per day, triggers, screens used
- Self-reported weekly hours
- **Narrated-versus-reported gap:** counted routine intensity against their stated hours
- Tools, alerts, spreadsheets, bots, scripts, people, paid or unpaid
- Anything used for this that was not built for this
- Range width, pair volatility, out-of-range frequency, intended hold period
- Previously ran something more demanding: yes or no, and why they stopped
- Suppressed demand: positions foregone, opportunities passed on
- Enjoyment: chore or genuinely liked
- Classification: `hidden_substitute`, `structural_avoidance`, or `normalized_cost`, with supporting quote
- Referral to a larger operator: yes or no
